Introduction
General Problem Interested in solving and studying minimization problems subject to constraints:

$latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & f_0(x)\\ \text{subject to} & x \in D \end{cases}, &s=3$

where
  • $latex x&s=3$ are problem parameters;
  • $latex f_0:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}&s=3$ is the objective function and $latex f_0(x) &s=3$ usually interpreted as cost for choosing $latex x &s=3$;
  • $latex D \subset\mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ is a constraint set often described "geometrically".
Example 1. Design Problem Interpretation Let
  • $latex x,y&s=3$=scalar-valued design variables
    (e.g., dimensions of manufactured object, yaw and pitch of jet).
  • $latex f_0(x,y)&s=3$ = penalty for choosing design $latex (x,y)&s=3$
    (e.g., cost in material, energy, time, deviation from desired path).
  • $latex D&s=3$ = design specifications
    (i.e., allowable/possible values for $latex (x,y)&s=3$).
    E.g., $latex D = \{ (x,y) : a<x<b, c<y<d\}&s=3$ specifies minimum and maximum design values.
Then the problem is to find optimal design values $latex (x,y)&s=3$ which minimize cost $latex f_0&s=3$ and satisfy the design specifications $latex (x,y) \in D&s=3$.


Example 2. Minimize function over ellipse Let
$latex f_0(x,y) = 1 - \frac{\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)}{2} &s=3$
$latex D = \{ (x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + 2 y^2 \leq 1 \} &s=3$.
Then the problem

$latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & 1-\frac{\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)}{2}\\ \text{subject to} & x^2 + 2 y^2 \leq 1 \end{cases} &s=3$

is a familiar kind of purely geometric optimization problem.
It has two solutions: $latex (x,y,f_0(x,y)) = (\pm 1, 0 , \frac{1}{2}) &s=3$.
Many applied problems can look like this, but with an applied interpretation.
Problems With Structure General optimization problems can be numerically inefficient to solve or analytically difficult, unless $latex f_0&s=3$ and $latex D&s=3$ have additional structure/properties.
Identifying nice structure/properties of problem $latex \implies &s=3$ problem may become analytically solvable or numerically efficient.
Examples of nice structure:
linearity
$latex f_0(x) = c^T x$
$latex D$ defined in terms of linear equalities/inequalities;
e.g., $latex a_1^T x \geq 0, \ldots, a_m^T x \geq 0$, or succinctly written $latex A x \succeq 0$.


convexity or quasiconvexity
E.g., convex if $latex f_0(tx+(1-t)y) \leq tf_0(x) + (1-t)f_0(y)$
$latex 0<t<1$
$latex D$ is itself a convex set.


sparsity or other matrix structure
E.g., $latex f_0(x) = x^T C x$ and $latex D$ given by $latex Ax \succeq 0$
If $latex C,A$ sparse
$latex \implies$ lots of cancellation
$latex \implies$ improve computational efficiency

Linear Programming Simplest case: $latex f_0&s=3$ is linear and $latex D&s=3$ defined in terms of linear constraints.
Notation: if $latex x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n &s=3$, then

$latex x \succeq y &s=3$ means $latex x_i \geq y_i &s=3$ for $latex i =1,\ldots, n &s=3$.

$latex c^T &s=3$ = transpose of the column vector $latex c \in \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$.
Linear program: given $latex c \in \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$, $latex b \in \mathbb{R}^m&s=3$, $latex A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}&s=3$, solve

$latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize}&c^T x\\ \text{subject to}& Ax \succeq b\\ &x \succeq 0 \end{cases}.&s=3$

Thus $latex f_0(x) = c^T x&s=3$ and $latex D = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: Ax \succeq b, x \succeq 0\}&s=3$.

Positives of linear programming:
  • Conceptually simple: relies heavily on linear algebra
  • There are classical numerical methods which are often very efficient.
  • If $latex x^\star \in D&s=3$ is local minimizer of $latex f_0&s=3$ on $latex D&s=3$, then it is automatically a global minimizer on $latex D&s=3$.
  • Can sometimes approximate smooth problems linearly; however, usually can only give "local" results.
    (E.g., $latex \sin(x) \sim x &s=3$ for $latex 0 \leq x \ll 1 &s=3$.)


Shortcomings of linear programming:
  • Many applied problems are not linear.
  • Many problems may not even be (suitably) approximated by linear programs.
    E.g., the "barrier"

    $latex I_{-}(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x <0\\ \infty & x\geq0 \end{cases} &s=3$

    is better approximated by the a "logarithmic barrier" of the form $latex - c \log(-x) &s=3$ than any linear function.
Convex Optimization Convex optimization problem: $latex f_0&s=3$ and $latex D&s=3$ are convex.
This is the main focus of the course.

Positives of Convex Optimization:
  • Relatively conceptually simple.
  • Still often have efficient, albeit more sophisticated, numerical methods.
  • Many applied problems may be recast as or approximated by convex optimization problems.
  • If $latex x^\star \in D&s=3$ is local minimizer of $latex f_0&s=3$ on $latex D&s=3$, then it is automatically a global minimizer on $latex D&s=3$.


Shortcomings of Convex Optimization:
  • Problems may seriously fail to be analytically tractable or numerically efficient.
  • Exists nonlinear problems which cannot be approximated by convex problems.
    • Example (Least Squares) A standard and ubiquitous kind of convex optimization problem is the least squares problem.
    This problem takes the form:

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & \Vert Ax-b \Vert^2\\ \text{subject to}& f_i(x) \leq 0, i=1,\ldots,m\\ &h_i(x) =0, i=1,\ldots,p \end{cases},&s=3$

    where
    • $latex \Vert \cdot \Vert&s=3$ is some norm
    • $latex A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}&s=3$ a matrix
    • $latex b \in \mathbb{R}^m&s=3$ a fixed vector
    • $latex f_0(x) = \Vert A x-b \Vert^2&s=3$ is the (convex) objective function
    • $latex f_i,h_i&s=3$ are convex.
    N.B.: will come back to this problem.

    Optimal Control Let
    $latex x(t),u(t),a(t),f(t) &s=3$ be functions $latex \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} &s=3$.
    Assume $latex x(t) &s=3$ evolves by

    $latex \begin{aligned} \dot x(t) &= a(t) x(t) + f(t) u(t)\\ \end{aligned} &s=3 $

    Here,
    • $latex \dot x(t) &s=3$ is the time derivative of $latex x(t) &s=3$.
    • $latex a(t),f(t) &s=3$ are assumed to be given;
    • we think of $latex x(t) &s=3$ as being the state of some system at time $latex t &s=3$;
    • we think of $latex u(t) &s=3$ as an input we are allowed to choose to dictate the evolution $latex x(t) &s=3$ of $latex x(0) &s=3$; i.e., $latex u(t) &s=3$ "controls" the system;
    • When $latex u(t)=u(x(t),t) &s=3$, the system experiences "feedback."
    • the goal: choose control law $latex u(t) = u(x(t),t) &s=3$ so that $latex x(t) &s=3$ is as "desirable" as possible.
    Example: Optimal Control Problems Optimal control problem: choose the "best" control $latex u(t) &s=3$ which gives the "most" desirable $latex x(t) &s=3$.
    Typically "best" and "desirable" are determined by size/cost of $latex u(t) &s=3$ and $latex x(t) &s=3$; e.g., one may wish to minimize

    $latex \int u(t)^2 + x(t)^2 dt. &s=3 $

    Therefore: problem is to solve (roughly speaking)

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & \int u(t)^2 + x(t)^2 dt\\ \text{subject to} & x'(t) = a(t) x(t) + f(t) u(t)\\ \end{cases} &s=3 $

    This will be another focus of the course and we will see some optimal control problems can be recast as convex optimization problems.

    Convex Geometry
    Convex Sets Convex set: a subset $latex X \subset \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ satisfying:

    for all $latex x,y \in X&s=3$ and $latex t \in [0,1]&s=3$, there holds $latex tx + (1-t) y \in X&s=3$.

    I.e., $latex X&s=3$ contains all line segments whose endpoints belong to $latex X&s=3$.

    Examples: some standard convex sets.

    1. Closed or open polytopes in $latex \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$.
      E.g., the interior of a tetrahedron.
    2. Euclidean balls, ellipsoids.
    3. Linear subspaces and affine spaces (e.g., lines, planes).
    4. Given a norm $latex \Vert \cdot \Vert&s=3$ on $latex \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$, the $latex \Vert \cdot \Vert&s=3$-ball

      $latex \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \Vert x - x_{0} \Vert \leq r \}&s=3$

      with center $latex x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ and radius $latex r>0&s=3$ is a convex set.
      Recall: a norm satisfies
      • $latex \Vert x+ y \Vert \leq \Vert x \Vert + \Vert y \Vert&s=3$ for all vectors $latex x,y &s=3$;
      • $latex \Vert c x \Vert = |c| \Vert x \Vert&s=3$ for all vectors $latex x &s=3$ and scalars $latex c &s=3$;
      • $latex \Vert x \Vert=0&s=3$ iff $latex x =0&s=3$.
    Affine Subsets Affine subset: A subset $latex X \subset \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ satisfying:

    For all $latex x,y \in X&s=3$ and $latex t \in \mathbb{R}&s=3$, there holds $latex tx + (1-t) y \in X&s=3$.

    I.e., $latex X&s=3$ contains all lines which pass through two distinct points in $latex X&s=3$.
    N.B.: An affine subset is just a translated linear subspace:
    "a linear space that's forgotten its origin".
    Example 1. Let $latex X = \{ (x,y,0): x,y \in \mathbb{R} \}&s=3$ be the $latex xy&s=3$-plane in $latex \mathbb{R}^3&s=3$.
    Then any translation or rotation of $latex X&s=3$ is an affine subset.
    Example 2. If $latex A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}&s=3$, $latex b \in \mathbb{R}^m&s=3$, then $latex X=\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: Ax = b\}&s=3$ is affine.
    ($latex X&s=3$ is just a translate of $latex \text{ker}\,A&s=3$.)

    Cones Cone: A subset $latex X \subset \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ satisfying:

    For all $latex x \in X&s=3$ and $latex t \geq 0&s=3$, there holds $latex tx \in X&s=3$.

    I.e., $latex X&s=3$ contains all "positive" rays emanating from the origin and passing through any of its points.
    Proposition. $latex X \subset \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ is a convex cone iff for all $latex x_1,x_2 \in X&s=3$ and $latex \theta_1,\theta_2\geq0&s=3$, there holds $latex \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2 \in X&s=3$.
    Proof.
    Step 1. ($latex \implies &s=3$) Suppose $latex X&s=3$ is a convex cone and let $latex x_1,x_2 \in X&s=3$ and $latex \theta_1,\theta_2\geq0&s=3$ be arbitrary.
    Want to show: $latex \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2 \in X &s=3$.
    Step 2. Being conic implies $latex x = \frac{\theta_1}{t} x_1&s=3$ and $latex y=\frac{\theta_2}{1-t}x_2&s=3$ belong to $latex X&s=3$ for all $latex 0<t<1&s=3$.
    Step 3. Being convex implies $latex tx+ (1-t)y = \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2 \in X&s=3$, as desired.
    Step 4. ($latex \impliedby &s=3$) Suppose $latex X&s=3$ is such that $latex \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2 \in X&s=3$ for all $latex x_1,x_2 \in X&s=3$ and $latex \theta_1,\theta_2\geq0&s=3$.
    Want to show: $latex X &s=3$ is a convex cone.
    Step 5. $latex X&s=3$ being conic follows from taking $latex \theta_1\geq0&s=3$ arbitrary and $latex \theta_2 = 0&s=3$.
    Step 6. Convexity follows from taking $latex \theta_1 + \theta_2 = 1&s=3$ with $latex \theta_1,\theta_2 \geq0&s=3$ and $latex 0 \leq \theta_1 \leq 1 &s=3$.
    Indeed: $latex \theta_1 + \theta_2 = 1 &s=3$ and $latex t := \theta_1 &s=3$ $latex \implies &s=3$ $latex t x_1 + (1-t) x_2 \in X &s=3$ for $latex 0 \leq t \leq 1 &s=3$ since $latex 1-t = \theta_2 &s=3$.

    1. Hyperplanes $latex \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^n : a^{T} x = 0\}&s=3$ with normal $latex a \in \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$,

      $latex \{ (x,t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} : \Vert x \Vert \leq t \}&s=3$,

      which is a convex cone in $latex \mathbb{R}^{n+1}&s=3$.

    Positive Semidefiniteness
    Symmetric matrix: a matrix $latex X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} &s=3$ satisfying $latex X = X^T &s=3$; i.e.,

    $latex X = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1n} \\ x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2n}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{nn} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{n1} \\ x_{12} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{n2}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ x_{1n} & x_{2n} & \cdots & x_{nn} \end{bmatrix} = X^T. &s=1$

    Set of symmetric matrices:

    $latex \boldsymbol{S}^n = \{ X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}: X = X^T \}.&s=3$


    Positive semidefinite matrix: $latex X \in \boldsymbol{S}^n &s=3$ satisfying $latex z^T X z \geq 0&s=3$ for all $latex z \in \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$.
    Equivalently, $latex X&s=3$ only has nonnegative eigenvalues.
    If $latex X \in \boldsymbol{S}^n &s=3$ is positive semidefinite, then write $latex X \succeq 0 &s=3$.
    If $latex X,Y \in \boldsymbol{S}^n &s=3$ and $latex X-Y\succeq0 &s=3$, then write $latex X \succeq Y &s=3$.
    Set of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices:

    $latex \boldsymbol{S}_+^n = \{ X \in \boldsymbol{S}^n: X \succeq 0 \}.&s=3$

    (N.B.: $latex \succeq&s=3$ is not the same as component-wise inequality, as was the case for vectors.)

    Positive definite matrix: $latex X \in \boldsymbol{S}_+^n &s=3$ satisfying $latex z^T X z = 0&s=3$ if and only if $latex z=0&s=3$.
    Equivalently, $latex X&s=3$ only has positive eigenvalues.
    If $latex X \in \boldsymbol{S}^n_+ &s=3$ is positive definite, then write $latex X \succ 0 &s=3$.
    If $latex X,Y \in \boldsymbol{S}^n_+ &s=3$ and $latex X-Y\succ0 &s=3$, then write $latex X \succ Y &s=3$.
    Set of symmetric positive definite matrices:

    $latex \boldsymbol{S}_{++}^n = \{ X \in \boldsymbol{S}^n: X \succ 0 \}.&s=3$

    Since $latex A &s=3$ has positive eigenvalues $latex \{2,4\} &s=3$, it follows that $latex A \succ 0 &s=3$.
    To see $latex A \succ 0 &s=3$ explicitly, observe

    $latex \begin{aligned} z^T A z &= \begin{bmatrix} z_1 & z_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} z_1 \\ z_2 \end{bmatrix}\\ &= 2 z_1^2 + 4 z_2^2\\ &\geq0 \end{aligned} &s=3$


    with $latex z^T A z = 0 &s=3$ iff $latex z = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} &s=3$.
    Example 2. Let $latex B = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}&s=3$.
    Since $latex B &s=3$ has nonnegative eigenvalues $latex \{ 4,0 \} &s=3$, it follows that $latex B \succeq 0 &s=3$.
    To see $latex B \succeq 0 &s=3$ explicitly, observe

    Evidently, $latex z^T B z=0 &s=3$ for $latex z = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\z_2 \end{bmatrix} &s=3$ and so $latex B \not\succ 0&s=3$.

    Positive Semidefinite Cone Proposition 1. $latex \boldsymbol{S}^n&s=3$ is a $latex \frac{n(n+1)}{2}&s=3$-dimensional real vector space and $latex \boldsymbol{S}_+^n&s=3$ is a convex cone in $latex \boldsymbol{S}^n&s=3$.
    Proof.
    Step 1. $latex \boldsymbol{S}^n&s=3$ is a vector space: if $latex X,Y \in \boldsymbol{S}^n&s=3$ and $latex c \in \mathbb{R}&s=3$, then it is easy to see:

    $latex (X+cY)^T = X^T + cY^T = X + cY&s=3$.

    and so $latex X+cY \in \boldsymbol{S}^n&s=3$.


    Step 2. $latex \text{dim}\,\boldsymbol{S}^n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}&s=3$: since $latex X \in \boldsymbol{S}^n&s=3$ implies $latex X=X^T&s=3$, we have the identification

    $latex \begin{aligned} X&= \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{x_{11}} & \boldsymbol{x_{12}} & \boldsymbol{x_{13}} & \cdots & \boldsymbol{x_{1n}}\\ x_{12} & \boldsymbol{x_{22}} & \boldsymbol{x_{23}} &\cdots & \boldsymbol{x_{2n}}\\ x_{13} & x_{23} & \boldsymbol{x_{33}} &\cdots & \boldsymbol{x_{3n}}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ x_{1n} & x_{2n} & x_{3n} & \cdots & \boldsymbol{x_{nn}}\\ \end{bmatrix}\\ &\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\iff\\ \xi&:= \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1n} & x_{22} & x_{23} & \cdots & x_{2n} & \cdots & x_{nn} \end{bmatrix}^T, \end{aligned} &s=3$

    where the bolded entries in $latex X&s=3$ indicate the unique contributions to making $latex \xi&s=3$.
    Counting the number of bold entries shows $latex \xi&s=3$ has $latex \frac{n(n+1)}{2}&s=3$ entries and hence $latex \xi \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}&s=3$.


    Step 3. $latex \boldsymbol{S}_+^n&s=3$ is a convex cone: For $latex \theta_1,\theta_2\geq0&s=3$, $latex X,Y \in \boldsymbol{S}_+^n&s=3$ and $latex z \in \mathbb{R}^n &s=3$ there holds

    $latex z^T(\theta_1 X +\theta_2 Y)z = \theta_1 z^T X z + \theta_2 z^T Y z \geq 0&s=3$,

    and so $latex \theta_1 X + \theta_2 Y \in \boldsymbol{S}_+^n &s=3$.
    By the proposition in Convex Geometry.Cones, we conclude the desired result.


    Proposition 2. $latex \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\b&c \end{bmatrix} \in \boldsymbol{S}_+^2&s=3$ iff

    $latex a,c\geq0&s=3$ and $latex \det \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\b&c \end{bmatrix} = ac - b^2 \geq 0&s=3$.

    Basics of Optimization Problems
    General Optimization Problems By an optimization problem (OP) we mean the following:

    $latex \text{(OP)} \begin{cases} \text{minimize } & f_0(x) \quad \text{(objective)}\\ \text{subject to }& f_i(x) \leq 0, \quad i=1,\ldots,m \quad \text{(inequality constraints)}\\ & h_i(x) = 0, \quad i=1,\ldots,p \quad \text{(equality constraints)} \end{cases}.&s=3$

    We call

    $latex f_0:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}&s=3$ the objective function;
    $latex x \in \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ the optimization variable or parameters;
    $latex f_i:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}&s=3$, $latex i=1,\ldots,m&s=3$, the inequality constraint functions; and
    $latex h_i:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}&s=3$, $latex i=1,\ldots,p&s=3$, the equality constraint functions.

    The domain of (OP) is the intersection

    $latex D = \bigcap_{i=0}^{m} \text{dom} \, f_i \cap \bigcap_{i=1}^{p} \text{dom} \, h_i&s=3$.

    Feasibility Consider an (OP) as above.
    Feasible point: those $latex x \in D&s=3$ satisfying

    $latex \begin{aligned} f_i(x)&\leq 0\quad\text{for } i =1,\ldots,m\\ h_i(x) &= 0 \text{ for }i=1,\ldots,p. \end{aligned} &s=3$

    Feasible set: the subset $latex F \subset D &s=3$ consisting of the feasible points.
    Feasible problem: A problem with nonempty feasible set, i.e., $latex F \neq \emptyset&s=3$.
    Infeasible problem: A problem with empty feasible set; i.e., there are no $latex x \in D &s=3$ which satisfy the inequality and equality constraints.

    Remark.
    1. A feasible problem need not have a solution; e.g., $latex f(x) = e^x &s=3$ has no minimizer nor minimum on $latex \mathbb{R} &s=3$.
    2. An infeasible problem never has a solution--there are no parameters $latex x &s=3$ to even test.

    Basics of Convex Optimization
    Convex Optimization Problems Abstract convex optimization problem: A problem involving minimizing a convex objective function on a convex set.
    Convex optimization problem: a problem of the form

    $latex \text{(COP)} \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & f_0(x)\\ \text{subject to} & f_i(x) \leq 0, i = 1,\ldots,m\\ & a_i^T x = b_i, i =1,\ldots, p \end{cases}, &s=3$

    where
    $latex f_0:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}&s=3$ and $latex f_i:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}&s=3$ are convex; and
    $latex a_i \in \mathbb{R}^n&s=3$ and $latex b_i \in \mathbb{R}&s=3$ are fixed.


    Some Remarks
    Remark 1. As defined, a (COP) is an (OP) in standard form; naturally, there are nonstandard form (OP)'s equivalent to (COP)'s.
    E.g., the abstract (COP)

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & f_0(x,y) \\ \text{subject to} & (x+y+1)^2 = 0 \end{cases} &s=3$

    is readily seen to be equivalent to the standard form (COP)

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & f_0(x,y) \\ \text{subject to}& \begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} + 1 = 0 \end{cases} &s=3$.



    Remark 2. We emphasize: the equality constraints are assumed to be affine constraints.
    Moreover, the equality constraints

    $latex a_i^T x = b_i , \quad i=1,\ldots,p &s=3$

    can be rewritten as

    $latex A x = b &s=3$,

    where

    $latex \begin{aligned} A &= \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T\\a_2^T\\\vdots\\a_p^T\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\\vdots\\b_p\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{p} \end{aligned} &s=3$.



    Remark 3. The affine assumption on the equality constraints can be lifted at the possible expense of an intractable theory/numerical analysis.
    E.g., if $latex h: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R} &s=3$ is quasilinear, then $latex h(x) = 0 &s=3$ defines a convex set.


    Remark 4. Generally, $latex h(x) &s=3$ convex does not imply the level set $latex h(x) = 0 &s=3$ is convex; e.g., $latex h(x,y)=x^2+y^2-1 &s=3$ gives a sphere.


    Remark 5. The common domain

    $latex D = \bigcap_{i=0}^m \text{dom}\, f_i&s=3$

    is convex since it is an intersection of convex sets.
    Optimality for Convex Optimization Problems Assume throughout that $latex f_0:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}&s=3$ is the objective function for some given (COP) and that $latex F &s=3$ is the feasible set.

    Proposition 1. If $latex x^\star &s=3$ is a feasible local minimizer for a (COP), then it is the global minimizer for the (COP).
    Proof. We will follow a proof by contradiction; i.e., we will show that assuming $latex x^\star &s=3$ is not a global minimizer leads to a contradiction.
    Step 1. $latex x^\star &s=3$ being a feasible local minimizer means $latex x^\star \in F &s=3$ and that there is a $latex R>0 &s=3$ such that

    $latex f_0(x^\star) = \inf\{ f_0(z) : z \in F, \quad \Vert x-x^\star \Vert_2 \leq R \} &s=3;$

    i.e., $latex f_0(x^\star) \leq f_0(z) &s=3$ for all $latex z \in F &s=3$ with a distance at most $latex R &s=3$ of $latex x^\star &s=3$.
    Step 2. Supposing $latex x^\star &s=3$ is not a global minimizer, then there exists $latex y \in F &s=3$ such that $latex f_0(y) < f_0(x^\star) &s=3$.
    By choice of $latex R &s=3$, there must also hold $latex \Vert{y-x^\star}\Vert_2 > R &s=3$.
    Step 3. Set

    $latex \begin{aligned} z &= (1-t)x^\star + ty\\ t &= \frac{R}{2\Vert y-x^\star \Vert_2}, \end{aligned} &s=3$

    noting that $latex t \in [0,1] &s=3$ by Step 2. and so $latex z \in F &s=3$ since $latex F &s=3$ is convex.
    It follows that

    $latex \begin{aligned} \Vert z - x^\star \Vert_2 &= \Vert (1-t)x^\star + ty - x^\star \Vert_2\\ &= \Vert t(y-x^\star ) \Vert_2\\ &= \frac{R}{2\Vert y-x^\star\Vert_2} \Vert y-x^\star\Vert_2\\ &= \frac{R}{2}\\ &< R \end{aligned} &s=3$

    Step 4. Since $latex z &s=3$ is a convex combination of feasible points, since $latex f_0 &s=3$ is convex and since $latex f_0(y)<f_0(x^\star) &s=3$, there holds

    $latex \begin{aligned} f_0(z) &= f_0((1-t)x^\star + ty) \\ &\leq (1-t)f_0(x^\star) + t f_0(y)\\ & < (1-t) f_0(x^\star) + t f_0(x^\star)\\ &= f_0(x^\star). \end{aligned} &s=3$

    But, since $latex x^\star &s=3$ minimizes $latex f_0 &s=3$ on

    $latex \{x \in F : \Vert x - x^\star \Vert_2 \leq R \}&s=3$

    and since

    $latex \Vert z - x^\star \Vert_2 \leq R &s=3$

    we also have

    $latex f_0(x^\star) \leq f_0(z)&s=3$.

    This is a contradiction and so $latex x^\star &s=3$ must be a global minimizer.
    Proposition 2. If $latex f_0 &s=3$ is differentiable on $latex F &s=3$, then $latex x^\star \in F &s=3$ is a minimizer iff for all $latex y \in F &s=3$ there holds

    $latex f_0(y) \geq f_0(x) + \nabla f_0(x)^T (y-x) &s=3$.

    Step 1.($latex \implies &s=3$) Suppose $latex x^\star &s=3$ is a minimizer and suppose for contradiction that

    $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star)^T(y-x^\star)<0 &s=3$

    for some $latex y \in F &s=3$.
    Set $latex z_t = ty+(1-t)x^\star &s=3$, noting that $latex z_t \in F &s=3$ since $latex F &s=3$ is convex.
    Using

    $latex \frac{d}{dt} f_0(z_t)|_{t=0} = \nabla f_0(x^\star)^T (y-x^\star) < 0 &s=3$,

    we conclude $latex f_0 &s=3$ is decreasing near $latex z_0 = x^\star &s=3$ in the direction $latex y-x^\star&s=3$ and so $latex f_0(z_t)< f_0(x^\star) &s=3$ for small $latex t &s=3$.
    Since $latex z_t \in F &s=3$, this contradicts $latex x^\star &s=3$ being a minimizer.
    Additional justification Since $latex z_t &s=3$ defines a line passing through $latex x^\star &s=3$ with direction $latex y-x^\star &s=3$, it follows that $latex \frac{d}{dt} f_0(z_t)|_{t=0} &s=3$ is the directional derivative in direction $latex (y-x^\star)&s=3$, i.e., $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star)^T(y-x^\star)&s=3$.
    Step 2.($latex \impliedby &s=3$) Supposing

    $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star)(y-x^\star) \geq 0 &s=3$

    for all $latex y \in F &s=3$ and using the first order characterization at $latex x^\star &s=3$, namely,

    $latex f_0(y) \geq f_0(x^\star) + \nabla f_0(x^\star)^T(y-x^\star) &s=3$

    we readily conclude

    $latex f_0(y) \geq f_0(x^\star) &s=3$

    for all $latex y \in F &s=3$; i.e., that $latex x^\star &s=3$ is a minimizer for the problem.
    Corollary In case $latex f_0 &s=3$ is differentiable and $latex F = \text{dom}\,f_0 &s=3$ (equivalently, there are no nontrivial constraints), $latex x^\star \in \text{dom}\,f_0 &s=3$ is a minimizer iff

    $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star) = 0 &s=3$.

    Proof. By Proposition 2., we have that $latex x^\star \in \text{dom}\,f_0 &s=3$ is a minimizer iff

    $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star)^T(y-x^\star) \geq 0 &s=3$

    for all $latex y \in \text{dom}\, f_0 &s=3$.
    Differentiability of $latex f_0 &s=3$ requires $latex \text{dom}\,f_0 &s=3$ is open and so, for small $latex t \in \mathbb{R} &s=3$, there holds

    $latex y: = x^\star - t \nabla f_0(x^\star) \in \text{dom}\,f_0 &s=3$.

    But then

    $latex \begin{aligned} \nabla f_0(x^\star)^T(y-x^\star) &= \nabla f_0(x^\star)^T(x^\star - t \nabla f_0(x^\star) - x^\star)\\ &= -t\nabla f_0(x^\star)^T\nabla f_0(x^\star)\\ &= -t \Vert \nabla f_0(x^\star) \Vert_2^2\\ &\geq 0, \end{aligned} &s=3$

    which is only possible for $latex t>0 &s=3$ iff $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star) = 0 &s=3$.

    is called a relaxation of the (BLP).

    $latex F \to \{x \in [0,1]^n: Gx \preceq h \} &s=3$

    $latex F \to \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: Gx \preceq h \} &s=3$.

    Example. Problem Given $latex m &s=3$ workers and $latex n &s=3$ locations with $latex m \leq n &s=3$,
    • assign each worker to work at some location
    • assign at most one worker to a location
    • minimize cost of operation and transportation
    Notational set up

    $latex \begin{aligned} c_j &= \text{cost to operate at location }j\\ c_{ij} &= \text{cost to transport worker } i \text{ to location }j\\ x_j &= \begin{cases} 0 & \text{ if location }j\text{ is not operating}\\ 1 & \text{ if location }j\text{ is operating} \end{cases}\\ x_{ij} &= \begin{cases} 0 & \text{ if worker }i \text{ is not working at location }j\\ 1 & \text{ if worker }i \text{ is working at location }j \end{cases}. \end{aligned} &s=3$

    Let

    $latex \begin{aligned} \text{Cost vectors} & \begin{cases} c &= (c_j) \in \mathbb{R}^n\\ C &= (c_{ij}) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \end{cases}\\ \text{Optimization variables} &\begin{cases} x &= (x_j) \in \{0,1\}^n\\ X &= (x_{ij}) \in \{0,1\}^{m \times n} \end{cases} \end{aligned}. &s=3$



    Construct objective function
    We find

    $latex \begin{aligned} \text{total operational cost }&= c^Tx = c_1x_1 + \cdots +c_nx_n\\ \text{total transportation cost }&= \text{tr}\,(C^TX) = \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n c_{ij}x_{ij}\\ \text{total cost }&= c^Tx + \text{tr}\,(C^TX). \end{aligned} &s=3$

    Thus the objective function is $latex f_0(x,X) = c^Tx + \text{tr}\,(C^TX)&s=3$.

    Construct constraints
    The constraint that $latex x_i,x_{ij}&s=3$ are binary is of course natural for the problem.
    Since each worker is selected only once, we have

    $latex \sum_{j=1}^n x_{ij} = 1, \quad \text{ for each } i=1,\ldots,m&s=3$.

    Lastly, observe $latex x_j=0 \implies x_{ij}=0 &s=3$ since the $latex j &s=3$th location not operating means it cannot host a worker; thus we have $latex x_{ij} \leq x_j &s=3$.

    Formulate Problem
    Putting everything together, the (BLP) formulation of the problem is

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & c^Tx + \text{tr}\,(C^TX)\\ \text{subject to} & x \in \{0,1\}^n\\ &X \in \{0,1\}^{m \times n}\\ &\sum_{j=1}^n x_{ij} =1, i = 1,\ldots,m\\ & x_{ij} \leq x_j, i=1,\ldots,m,j=1,\ldots,n \end{cases}. &s=3$

    An (LP) relaxation (and hence convex approximation) of this (BLP) is

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & c^Tx + \text{tr}\,(C^TX)\\ \text{subject to} & x \in [0,1]^n\\ &X \in [0,1]^{m \times n}\\ &\sum_{j=1}^n x_{ij} =1, i = 1,\ldots,m\\ & x_{ij} \leq x_j, i=1,\ldots,m,j=1,\ldots,n \end{cases}. &s=3$

    $latex \text{(QP)} \begin{cases} \text{minimize}&\frac{1}{2}x^TQx + q^Tx\\ \text{subject to} &Gx \preceq h\\ &Ax =b \end{cases} &s=3$

    where

    $latex \begin{aligned} &Q \in \boldsymbol{S}^n, \quad G \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}, \quad A \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}\\ &q \in \mathbb{R}^n, \quad h \in \mathbb{R}^m,\quad b \in \mathbb{R}^p. \end{aligned} &s=3$

    Remarks.
    1. As for (LP), the constraints

      $latex \begin{cases} Gx \preceq h\\ Ax =b \end{cases} &s=3$

      describe a polyhedron.
    2. Given $latex d \in \mathbb{R} &s=3$, then

    3. If

      $latex f_0(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^TQx + q^Tx &s=3$,

      then

      $latex \begin{aligned} \nabla f_0(x) &= Qx + q\\ \nabla^2 f_0(x) &= Q. \end{aligned} &s=3$

      Thus $latex Q \succeq 0 &s=3$ implies $latex f_0 &s=3$ is convex.
      N.B.: The factor $latex \frac{1}{2} &s=3$ is just a convenient normalization.
    4. The generalization

      $latex \begin{aligned} &Gx \preceq h \, \to \, \frac{1}{2} x^T Q_ix+g_i^Tx \leq h_i \\ &Q_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, g_i \in \mathbb{R}^n, h_i \in \mathbb{R}, \quad i =1,\ldots,m \\ \end{aligned} &s=3$

      results in quadratically constrained quadratic programming (QCQP).
      Imposing $latex Q,Q_i \in \boldsymbol{S}_+^n &s=3$ ensures the (QCQP) is convex.
      (C.f., Convex Function Theory.Level Sets.)
    5. The generalization

      $latex \begin{aligned} &Ax=b \, \to \, \frac{1}{2} x^T P_ix+a_i^Tx =b_i \\ &P_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, a_i \in \mathbb{R}^n, b_i \in \mathbb{R}, \quad i =1,\ldots,m \\ \end{aligned} &s=3$

      also results in a (QCQP), but this can break convexity.

      $latex x_i(x_i-1)=0 \text{ or } x_i^2 = 1 &s=3$

      result in a (BLP) since these constraints enforce $latex x_i \in \{0,1\} &s=3$ or $latex x_i \in \{-1,1\}&s=3$, respectively.
    6. There holds

      $latex \begin{aligned} \text{(QCQP)} + Q_i = P_i= 0 &\iff (QP)\\ (QP) + Q=0 &\iff (LP). \end{aligned} &s=3$

      Indeed, first note that, since $latex x^TQx &s=3$ is a scalar, we have

      $latex x^TQx = (x^TQx)^T = x^TQ^Tx &s=3$.

      $latex f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^TQx + q^T x &s=3$.

      $latex \begin{aligned} f(x) &= \frac{f(x) + f(x)}{2}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{2}x^T Q x + q^Tx + \frac{1}{2}x^T Q^T x + q^Tx \right)\\ &=\frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{2}x^T(Q+Q^T)x + 2 q^Tx \right)\\ &=:\frac{1}{2}x^T \tilde{Q} x + q^T x, \end{aligned} &s=3$

      where

      $latex \tilde{Q} = \frac{1}{2}(Q+Q^T) &s=3$.

      $latex \tilde{Q}^T = \left( \frac{1}{2}(Q+Q^T) \right)^T = \frac{1}{2}(Q^T+Q) = \tilde{Q}&s=3$.



      Thus every quadratic

      $latex f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^TQx + q^Tx &s=3$

      has a "symmetric representation" of the form

      $latex f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^T\tilde{Q}x + q^Tx &s=3$ with $latex \tilde{Q} \in \boldsymbol{S}^n&s=3$.

    $latex \begin{aligned} A &= \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}, \quad a_1,a_2,a_3 \in \mathbb{R}^2 \\ x &= \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} &s=3$

    N.B.: a set of three 2-dimensional vectors is always linearly dependent and so

    $latex a_3 = c_1 a_1 + c_2 a_2 &s=3$

    for some $latex c_1,c_2 \in \mathbb{R}&s=3$
    Therefore, we may write

    $latex A=: \begin{bmatrix} a & \alpha & u \\ b & \beta & v \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & \alpha & c_1 a + c_2 \alpha\\ b & \beta & c_1 b + c_2 \beta \end{bmatrix} &s=3$.



    Computing

    $latex \begin{aligned} Ax &= \begin{bmatrix} a & \alpha & c_1 a + c_2 \alpha\\ b & \beta & c_1 b + c_2 \beta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}\\ &= \begin{bmatrix} ax_1 + \alpha x_2 + c_1 a x_3 + c_2 \alpha x_3\\ bx_1 + \beta x_2 + c_1 b x_3 + c_2 \beta x_3 \end{bmatrix}\\ &= \begin{bmatrix} a(x_1 + c_1 x_3) + \alpha (x_2 + c_2 x_3)\\ b(x_1 + c_1 x_3) + \beta(x_2 + c_2 x_3) \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} &s=3$

    we see that minimizing

    $latex \Vert Ax-b \Vert_2^2 = \bigg\Vert \begin{bmatrix} a(x_1 + c_1 x_3) + \alpha (x_2 + c_2 x_3)\\ b(x_1 + c_1 x_3) + \beta(x_2 + c_2 x_3) \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} b_1\\b_2 \end{bmatrix} \bigg\Vert_2^2 &s=2$

    is equivalent to minimizing

    $latex \begin{aligned} \Vert \hat{A}y - b \Vert_2^2 &:= \bigg\Vert \begin{bmatrix} a&\alpha\\b&\beta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}y_1\\y_2\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{bmatrix} \bigg\Vert_2^2\\ &= \bigg\Vert \begin{bmatrix} ay_1 + \alpha y_2\\ by_1 + \beta y_2 \end{bmatrix} -\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{bmatrix} \bigg\Vert_2^2 \end{aligned}. &s=3$

    Equivalence follows from the change of variables

    $latex \begin{aligned} y_1 &= x_1 + c_1 x_3\\ y_2 &= x_2 + c_2 x_3 \end{aligned} &s=3$.



    Therefore, a (LS) problem with matrix of size $latex 2 \times 3 &s=3$ is equivalent to a (LS) problem with matrix of size $latex 2 \times 2 &s=3$.

    This argument holds in general: if $latex A &s=3$ has linearly dependent columns, can use change of variables to ensure linear independence.
    Remarks.
    1. If $latex Ax=b &s=3$ has solution $latex x^\star &s=3$, then $latex x^\star &s=3$ solves the (LS) problem.

    2. If $latex Ax=b &s=3$ has no solution, then any $latex x^\star &s=3$ solving the (LS) problem gives a "best" estimate solution to $latex Ax = b&s=3$, where "best" is chosen to mean in terms of the vector norm $latex \Vert \cdot \Vert_2 &s=3$.

    3. While minimizing $latex \Vert \cdot \Vert_2^2 &s=3$ is equivalent to minimizing $latex \Vert \cdot \Vert_2 &s=3$, the exponent $latex 2 &s=3$ ensures $latex \Vert \cdot \Vert_2^2 &s=3$ is differentiable (at $latex 0 &s=3$).
      (C.f.: $latex |x|&s=3$ is not differentiable at $latex x=0 &s=3$, but $latex x^2 &s=3$ is.)
    Solving the (LS)
    Step 1. (The problem is convex) The objective function

    $latex f_0(x) = \Vert Ax-b\Vert_2^2 = x^TA^TAx - 2b^TAx+b^Tb &s=3$

    is convex since

    $latex \nabla^2 f_0 = 2 A^TA \succeq 0 &s=3$.

    To see $latex A^TA \succeq 0 &s=3$, observe:
    1. $latex (A^T A)^T = A^T A &s=3$ and so $latex A^TA \in \boldsymbol{S}^n &s=3$, i.e., $latex A^TA &s=3$ is symmetric;
    2. for all $latex z \in \mathbb{R}^n &s=3$ there holds

      $latex z^T A^T A z = (Az)^T Az = \Vert Az \Vert_2^2 \geq 0 &s=3$,

      i.e., $latex A^TA \in \boldsymbol{S}_+^n &s=3$.
    Step 2. (The critical points) We compute

    $latex \begin{aligned} \nabla f_0 &= \nabla (x^T A^TA x) - \nabla(2b^TAx) + \nabla(b^Tb)\\ &= 2 A^TAx - 2A^Tb + 0\\ &= 2(A^TAx - A^Tb). \end{aligned} &s=3$

    Therefore, $latex \nabla f_0 = 0 &s=3$ iff

    $latex A^TAx = A^T b &s=3$.

    This system of equations consists of what are called the normal equations.
    Step 3. (The solution) By corollary proved above: $latex x^\star &s=3$ is a solution iff $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star) = 0 &s=3$.
    Moreover, $latex A &s=3$ having linearly independent columns ensures that $latex A^TA &s=3$ is invertible:
    1. linear independent columns $latex \implies &s=3$ $latex Az=0&s=3$ iff $latex z=0&s=3$.
    2. $latex A^TAz = 0 &s=3$ $latex \iff &s=3$ $latex Az=0 &s=3$ $latex \iff &s=3$ $latex z = 0 &s=3$.
    3. Since $latex A^TA &s=3$ is square, can conclude $latex A^TA &s=3$ is invertible.


    Lastly: since

    $latex \nabla f_0(x^\star)= 0 &s=3$ iff $latex A^TA x^\star = A^T b&s=3$

    and since $latex A^TA &s=3$ is invertible, we conclude that the solution to the (LS) is

    $latex x^\star = (A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb &s=3$.

    $latex \begin{aligned} K_1 &= \{ G_1 x \preceq h_1 \}\\ K_2 &= \{ G_2 x \preceq h_2 \}, \end{aligned} &s=3$

    then the (NPP) for $latex K_1,K_2 &s=3$ is the (QP) given by

    which is again a convex function.

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize}&f_0(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_0} c_{0k} x_1^{a_{01k}}\cdots x_n^{a_{0nk}}\\ \text{subject to}& f_i(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_i} c_{ik} x_1^{a_{i1k}} \cdots x_n^{a_{ink}} \leq 1, \, i=1,\ldots,m\\ & h_i(x) =d_{i}x_1^{b_{i1}}\cdots x_n^{b_{in}} = 1, \, i=1,\ldots,p \end{cases}. &s=3$

    2. The constraints are equivalent since $latex \log &s=3$ is monotonic and injective on $latex \mathbb{R}_{++}&s=3$.

    $latex \text{diag}(v) = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & v_2 &\cdots &0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & v_m \end{bmatrix}. &s=3$

    $latex v \succeq 0 \iff \text{diag}(v) \succeq 0&s=3$.

    (Indeed, the eigenvalues of $latex \text{diag}(v) &s=3$ are the components of $latex v &s=3$.) Therefore,

    $latex \begin{aligned} Ax+b \succeq 0 &\iff \text{diag}(Ax+b) \succeq 0 \\ \text{(vector inequality)} & \iff \text{(matrix inequality)}. \end{aligned} &s=3$



    Letting

    $latex A=\begin{bmatrix}a_1 & \cdots & a_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad a_i \in \mathbb{R}^m &s=3$,

    we have

    $latex Ax + b = b + x_1 a_1 + \cdots + x_n a_n &s=3$.



    Therefore, using

    $latex \begin{aligned} \text{diag}(v+\lambda w) &= \begin{bmatrix} v_1 + \lambda w_1 &0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & v_2 + \lambda w_2 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & v_n + \lambda w_n \end{bmatrix}\\ &= \begin{bmatrix} v_1 &0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & v_2 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & v_n \end{bmatrix} + \lambda \begin{bmatrix} w_1 &0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & w_2 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & w_n \end{bmatrix}\\ &= \text{diag}(v) + \lambda \text{diag}(w) \end{aligned} &s=3$

    we have

    $latex \begin{aligned} \text{diag}(Ax + b) &= \text{diag}(b + x_1 a_1 + \cdots + x_n a_n)\\ &= \text{diag}(b) + x_1 \text{diag}(a_1) + \cdots + x_n \text{diag}(a_n). \end{aligned} &s=3$



    Therefore, defining

    $latex \begin{aligned} F_0 &= \text{diag}(b), \quad F_i = \text{diag}(a_i)\\ F(x) &= F_0 + x_1 F_1 + \cdots + x_n F_n = \text{diag}(Ax+b), \end{aligned} &s=3$

    we conclude

    $latex \begin{aligned} Ax+b \succeq 0 \iff \text{diag}(Ax+b) \succeq 0 \iff F(x) \succeq 0 \end{aligned} &s=3$.



    In conclusion, we have that the (LP)

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & c^Tx\\ \text{subject to} & Ax + b \succeq 0 \end{cases}, &s=3$

    is equivalent to the (SDP)

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & c^Tx\\ \text{subject to} & F(x)= \text{diag}(Ax+b) \succeq 0 \end{cases}. &s=3$

    Example 2. Nonlinear OP as a SDP Consider the nonlinear (COP)

    $latex \text{(OP1)} \begin{cases} \text{minimize}& \frac{(c^Tx)^2}{d^Tx}\\ \text{subject to} & Ax + b \succeq 0\\ & d^Tx > 0 \end{cases} &s=3$

    where

    $latex \begin{aligned} &c,d \in \mathbb{R}^n\\ &A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}, b \in \mathbb{R}^m. \end{aligned} &s=3$

    We will recast (OP1) as a (SDP).
    N.B.:
    1. $latex d^Tx > 0 \implies &s=3$ choice of domain (a halfspace) of objective and ensures convexity.
    2. $latex d^Tx < 0 \implies &s=3$ concave problem.
    To begin, recall we may recast problem in epigraph form:

    $latex \text{(OP1)} \begin{cases} \text{minimize}& t\\ \text{subject to} & Ax + b \succeq 0\\ & d^Tx > 0\\ & \frac{(c^Tx)^2}{d^Tx} \leq t \end{cases}. &s=3$

    N.B.: this introduces the new optimization variable $latex t &s=3$. Goal: find a symmetric matrix-valued function

    $latex F(x,t) = F_0 + x_1F_1 + \cdots + x_nF_n + t F_{n+1} &s=3$

    such that the constraints

    $latex \begin{cases} &Ax + b \succeq 0\\ & d^Tx > 0\\ & \frac{(c^Tx)^2}{d^Tx} \leq t \end{cases} &s=3$

    may be recast as the LMI

    $latex \begin{cases} F(x) \succeq 0 \end{cases}. &s=3$

    Idea: we know

    $latex Ax + b \succeq 0 \iff \text{diag}(Ax+b) \succeq 0 &s=3$.

    On the other hand:

    $latex \begin{aligned} \frac{(c^Tx)^2}{d^Tx} \leq t &\iff (c^Tx)^2 \leq td^Tx \\ &\iff td^Tx - (c^Tx)^2 \geq0 \end{aligned} &s=3$.

    Recall: if $latex \gamma>0 &s=3$, then

    $latex \begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{bmatrix}\succeq0 \iff \alpha\geq0, \alpha\gamma - \beta^2 \geq 0&s=3$

    Therefore, given $latex d^Tx>0&s=3$, we have

    $latex \begin{aligned} \frac{(c^Tx)^2}{d^Tx} \leq t & \iff \begin{bmatrix}t & c^Tx\\ c^Tx & d^Tx \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0 \end{aligned} &s=3$.

    Using that

    $latex \begin{bmatrix} A &\vline &0\\ \hline 0 & \vline& B \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0 \iff A \succeq 0 , B \succeq 0, &s=3$

    we therefore introduce

    $latex E = \begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(Ax+b) & 0 & 0\\ 0 & t & c^Tx\\ 0 & c^Tx & d^Tx \end{bmatrix} &s=3$

    to capture the problems constraints.
    Indeed, evidently, $latex E \succeq 0 &s=3$ iff

    $latex \text{diag}(Ax+b) \succeq 0 &s=3$ and $latex \begin{bmatrix} t & c^tx\\c^tx & d^tx \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0 &s=3$.

    Therefore,

    $latex \begin{array}{l} Ax + b \succeq 0\\ d^Tx > 0\\ \frac{(c^Tx)^2}{d^Tx} \leq t \end{array} \iff \begin{array}{l} \begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(Ax+b) & 0 & 0\\ 0 & t & c^Tx\\ 0 & c^Tx & d^Tx \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0 \end{array} &s=3$.

    $latex A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & \cdots & a_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad a_i \in \mathbb{R}^m&s=3$

    and $latex (m+2)\times(m+2) &s=3$ matrices

    $latex \begin{aligned} F_0 &= \text{diag}(b,0,0) \\ F_i & = \begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(a_i) &0 &0\\ 0&0&c_i\\ 0&c_i&d_i \end{bmatrix}\\ F_{n+1} & = \begin{bmatrix}0_{m \times m} &0 &0\\0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\\ \end{aligned}. &s=3$

    Then

    $latex \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(Ax+b) & 0 & 0\\ 0 & t & c^Tx\\ 0 & c^Tx & d^Tx \end{bmatrix} = F_0 + x_1F_1 + \cdots + x_nF_n + t F_{n+1}:=F(x,t) \end{aligned} &s=3$

    and so (OP1) is equivalent to the (SDP)

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize}& t\\ \text{subject to} & F(x,t) \succeq 0. \end{cases} &s=3$

    Lagrangian Duality

    Throughout, let (OP) denote a given optimization problem of the form

    $latex \text{(OP)} \begin{cases} \text{minimize} & f_0(x)\\ \text{subject to} & f_i(x) \leq 0 , \quad i=1,\ldots,m\\ & h_i(x) = 0, \quad i = 1,\ldots,p \end{cases}. &s=3$

    Recall:

    $latex \begin{aligned} \text{Problem domain: }& D := \bigcap_{i=0}^m \text{dom}\, f_i \cap \bigcap_{i=1}^p \text{dom}\,h_i\\ \text{Optimal value: } & p^\star := \inf\{f_0(x): x \in D , x \text{ feasible}\}. \end{aligned} &s=3$

    The Lagrange Dual Lagrangian: the function

    $latex \begin{aligned} L&:\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R} \\ \text{dom}\,L &= D \times \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^p \end{aligned} &s=3$

    given by

    $latex \begin{aligned} L(x,\lambda,\nu) = f_0(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i f_i(x) + \sum_{i=1}^p \nu_i h_i(x) \end{aligned} &s=3$.

    N.B.: for each fixed $latex x \in D &s=3$, the function

    $latex (\lambda,\nu) \mapsto L(x,\lambda,\mu)&s=3$

    is affine.

    Lagrange multipliers: the variables $latex \lambda_i &s=3$ and $latex \nu_i &s=3$.
    The vectors

    $latex \begin{aligned} \lambda := \begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1\\\lambda_2\\\vdots\\\lambda_m\end{bmatrix}, \quad \nu := \begin{bmatrix}\nu_1\\\nu_2\\\vdots\\\nu_p\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} &s=3$

    are called dual variables.

    Lagrange dual function: the function

    $latex \begin{aligned} g&:\mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\\ \end{aligned} &s=3$

    given by

    $latex g(\lambda,\nu) = \inf\{L(x,\lambda,\nu): x \in D\} &s=3$.

    Those $latex (\lambda,\nu) \in \mathbb{R}^m_+ \times \mathbb{R}^m &s=3$ satisfying

    $latex g(\lambda,\nu) > -\infty &s=3$

    are called dual feasible.
    N.B.: as an infimum of affine functions, $latex g &s=3$ is automatically concave.

    Proposition. For

    $latex \lambda \in \mathbb{R}^m_+, \quad \nu \in \mathbb{R}^p &s=3$,

    there holds

    $latex g(\lambda,\nu) \leq p^\star&s=3$,

    where $latex p^\star &s=3$ is the optimal value for the given (OP).
    Proof.
    1. Let $latex x \in D &s=3$ be feasible.
      Then

      $latex \begin{aligned} f_i(x) &\leq 0, \quad i=1,\ldots,m\\ h_i(x) &=0, \quad i=1,\ldots,p. \end{aligned} &s=3$



    2. Let $latex \lambda \succeq 0 &s=3$ and $latex \nu &s=3$ be arbitrary.
      Then feasibility of $latex x&s=3$ implies

      $latex \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i f_i(x) + \sum_{i=1}^p \nu_i h_i(x) = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i f_i(x) \leq 0. \end{aligned} &s=3$

      Consequently,

      $latex \begin{aligned} L(x,\lambda,\nu) = f_0(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i f_i(x) + \sum_{i=1}^p \nu_i h_i(x) \leq f_0(x). \end{aligned} &s=3$



    3. Therefore, for all feasible $latex x &s=3$, for $latex \lambda \succeq 0&s=3$ and for arbitrary $latex \nu &s=3$, there holds

      $latex \begin{aligned} g(\lambda,\nu) = \inf\{L(z,\lambda,\nu):z \in D \} \leq L(x,\lambda,\nu) \leq f_0(x) \end{aligned} &s=3$

      and so

      $latex g(\lambda,\nu) \leq p^\star &s=3$.

      (Indeed, $latex g(\lambda,\nu) &s=3$ is a lower bound of $latex f_0(x) &s=3$ and $latex p^\star &s=3$ is the greatest lower bound of $latex f_0(x) &s=3$.)


    Lagrangian as underestimator.
    (See CO 5.1.4)
    Define the indicator functions

    $latex I_-(t)= \begin{cases} 0 &t \leq 0\\ +\infty & t> 0 \end{cases},\qquad I_0(t)= \begin{cases} 0 & t=0\\ +\infty & t\neq0 \end{cases}. &s=3$

    Then

    $latex \begin{aligned} I_-(f_i(x)) &\text{ indicates when the constraint }f_i \text{ is active}\\ I_0(h_i(x)) &\text{ indicates when the constraint }h_i \text{ is active} \end{aligned} &s=3$

    and the (OP) is equivalent to

    $latex \begin{aligned} \begin{cases} \text{ minimize } f_0(x) + \sum_{i=1}^mI_-(f_i(x)) + \sum_{i=1}^p I_0(h_i(x)). \end{cases} \end{aligned} &s=3$

    $latex \begin{aligned}\sum_{i=1}^mI_-(f_i(x)) + \sum_{i=1}^p I_0(h_i(x)) \end{aligned} &s=3$

    $latex \begin{aligned} \lambda_i f_i(x) &\leq I_-(f_i(x))\\ \nu_i h_i(x) &\leq I_0(h_i(x)) \end{aligned} &s=3$

    $latex \begin{cases} \text{minimize } L(x,\lambda,\nu) \end{cases} &s=3$
